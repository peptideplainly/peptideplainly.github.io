Dosing & timing · Evidence · FAQ

What is BPC-157?

BPC-157 ("Body Protection Compound-157") is a synthetic peptide of 15 amino acids, originally derived from a protein found in stomach juice. It's popular online for injury recovery.

Is BPC-157 FDA-approved?

Not an FDA-approved drug for any use. It is banned at all times in sport: the 2026 WADA Prohibited List names BPC-157 under S0 (non-approved substances). In 2023 the FDA listed BPC-157 among bulk substances that "may present significant safety risks" for compounding, noting limited human safety information. In April 2026 it was removed from that category after its nomination was withdrawn, which does not mean it was approved. Its compounding status is under FDA advisory-committee review (FDA page). It is widely sold online as a "research chemical."

Checked against official sources on October 16, 2026. Rules vary by country and can change.

BPC-157 dosage: what we actually know

There is no established or approved human dose for BPC-157, so we don't give one. Here's what the research shows instead:

  • Human reports: the 2026 systematic review (PMID 42885452) found only three: knee joint injections (16 people), bladder instillation (12 people), and an IV safety pilot (2 people). These are very different routes, and the abstract doesn't report doses.
  • Animal studies: most BPC-157 research is in rats and mice (PMID 40756949), dosed by body weight. That doesn't convert neatly to people.

Why the numbers online don't agree

  • Many online "protocols" are converted from animal studies, where doses are set by body weight in rats or mice. Those don't translate directly to people.
  • The few human reports use different routes and settings, with no agreed standard.
  • Unregulated products can vary in what they actually contain.
  • Sellers and influencers often share numbers without sources.

What the human evidence says

  • Systematic review, 2026 (Acta Orthop Belg, PMID 42885452): found only 3 human studies with 30 people in total, all poor quality. The certainty of evidence was rated very low. The authors advise against clinical use outside regulated trials.
  • Systematic review, 2025 (HSS Journal, PMID 40756949): 36 studies, almost all in animals. The single human study cited reported that 7 of 12 people with knee pain felt relief after one injection. The authors note there is no clinical safety data in humans.

Common myths

  • Myth: "it's proven to heal tendons." Healing results come mostly from animal studies. Human data is tiny and low quality.
  • Myth: "no side effects = safe." No reported side effects in 30 people isn't evidence of safety, and products bought online may not contain what the label says.

BPC-157 FAQ

What is the BPC-157 dosage?

There is no established human dose. The few human reports used different routes, and most research is in animals. Talk to a licensed clinician.

Is BPC-157 FDA-approved?

No. It isn't approved by the FDA for any use and is sold as a research compound. WADA lists it as prohibited in sport.

Does BPC-157 work in humans?

A 2026 systematic review found only 3 small, low-quality human studies with 30 people in total. Most reported improvement, but the certainty of evidence was rated very low.

Is BPC-157 safe?

There's very little human safety data. No reported side effects in 30 people isn't proof of safety, and product quality can vary.

Why do BPC-157 doses online differ so much?

Most numbers come from animal studies or anecdotes, not human trials, so there's no agreed standard.
