BPC-157 and the HHS peptide compounding policy

The HHS peptide policy was published in the Federal Register on Fri, Oct 30, after clearing White House review on Oct 27. Here's what it does, what it doesn't, and where BPC-157 stands.

The short version: it's an interim, non-binding promise not to enforce, for state-licensed 503A pharmacies, federal facilities and licensed physicians compounding certain peptides. It is not an FDA approval, and it does not make "research use only" vials legal.

What the notice says

  • Title: "Enforcement Discretion Policy Regarding 503A Compounding Using Certain Peptide Bulk Drug Substances," Federal Register doc 2026-22192, Docket No. HHS-OASH-2026-0496, RIN 0910-ZD94, from the HHS Office of the Secretary (Federal Register public inspection).
  • Review: OIRA concluded its review on Oct 27, 2026: "Consistent with Change" (reginfo.gov). It was received Oct 14 under the working title "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides."
  • Publication: published Oct 30, 2026, 91 FR 68526 (Federal Register).
  • Who it covers: HHS "does not intend to take enforcement action against a State-licensed pharmacy, Federal facility, or licensed physician compounding a drug product using a peptide bulk drug substance found in Appendix A" (Federal Register).
  • Interim and non-binding: HHS "may revisit application of this interim policy (in whole or in part) if, among other things, a safety signal or product quality issue arises," and the policy "is not binding on HHS or the public" (Federal Register).
  • Not an approval: "Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved," the notice says (Federal Register).
  • Comments: the notice says "until 2 weeks after date of publication"; regulations.gov lists the deadline as Nov 13, 2026, 11:59 p.m. ET, docket HHS-OASH-2026-0496 (regulations.gov).

Which peptides?

Six, listed in Appendix A of the HHS policy document (HHS policy PDF; the file name says "appendix-b"): BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500. We checked the list against the HHS document on Oct 31, 2026, and it matches what NBC News reported (NBC News). Appendix B limits each peptide to specific forms and routes.

The policy only applies when a set of conditions is met, including a patient-specific prescription, a bulk substance from an FDA-registered facility with a valid certificate of analysis, a single active ingredient per product, the label statement "This is a compounded drug product. Not for resale.", and reporting of serious adverse events and quality problems to FDA's MedWatch (HHS policy).

What it covers / doesn't cover

Covers

  • Compounding by a state-licensed pharmacy, federal facility or licensed physician (Federal Register).
  • Six peptides in Appendix A: BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax, TB-500 (HHS policy).
  • Only the forms in Appendix B: BPC-157 acetate (subcutaneous, intramuscular, oral, topical); TB-500 acetate and MOTS-c acetate (subcutaneous or intramuscular injection); epitalon acetate (subcutaneous or intramuscular injection, in intermittent courses); semax free base (intranasal); KPV acetate (topical, oral) (HHS policy).
  • Only when conditions are met, including a patient-specific prescription, an FDA-registered source with a valid certificate of analysis, a "compounded drug product" label and MedWatch reporting (HHS policy).

Doesn't cover

  • Blends or stacks: each product must contain "only a single Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) listed in Appendix A" (HHS policy).
  • Forms Appendix B doesn't list, such as oral epitalon, injectable KPV or injectable semax. Oral products also need documentation of adequate oral bioavailability in humans (HHS policy).
  • "Research use only" vials: the policy covers compounding by pharmacies, federal facilities and physicians; HHS says it aims to "discourage the continued, widespread use of certain research-use-only peptides" (HHS policy).
  • FDA approval: "Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved" (Federal Register).
  • State law: the notice doesn't address state law, state boards of pharmacy or preemption (Federal Register).

What it doesn't do

  • It doesn't approve BPC-157. None of the six peptides is FDA-approved.
  • It doesn't make "research use only" vials legal. It covers compounding by pharmacies, federal facilities and physicians, not research-chemical sellers. HHS says the policy is meant to "discourage the continued, widespread use of certain research-use-only peptides supplied by foreign manufacturers that pose a safety risk to US patients" (HHS policy).
  • It doesn't add anything to the 503A bulks list. The notice says FDA's future rulemakings "may propose" adding some or all of these peptides.
  • It doesn't change the evidence. BPC-157's research is still mostly animal studies (our guide).

Where BPC-157 stands today

  • Not FDA-approved for any use (our FDA list).
  • Not on category 1 of FDA's 503A bulk substance categories (FDA 503A categories).
  • The July 2026 advisory vote was non-binding. FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted to recommend BPC-157 and five others (FDA meeting page; vote report).

The pushback: Doctors for America

On Oct 16, Doctors for America urged OMB to return the policy to FDA (DFA statement). Their main points, in their words:

  • The policy "would let compounding pharmacies sell injectable peptides that have never been shown to be safe … or even reliably identified."
  • "In July, FDA's own scientists recommended that none of the seven peptides reviewed be added to the compounding list," and the committee "overrode them on narrow 8–6 votes."
  • On BPC-157 evidence, they cite a 2025 HSS Journal systematic review that found 35 animal studies and one small retrospective human report (12 patients with knee pain, 7 reporting relief).
  • They want any addition to go through proposed rule, public comment and final rule, with FDA's evidence reviews published.

Who wants it: telehealth

Telehealth company Hims & Hers has said it plans to offer peptides if regulations permit (Reuters). Peptides have also been promoted by HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. (Reuters).

What happens next

Comments close Nov 13, 2026. HHS can revisit the policy at any time, and FDA's future rulemakings "may propose" adding some or all of the six to the 503A bulks list. We track changes on our FDA status tracker.

BPC-157 policy FAQ

Is BPC-157 now legal to compound?

Only in a narrow way. HHS's enforcement-discretion policy was published in the Federal Register on Oct 30, 2026 (91 FR 68526; Federal Register). HHS says it does not intend to take enforcement action against "a State-licensed pharmacy, Federal facility, or licensed physician" compounding with a peptide listed in Appendix A, when the policy's conditions are met. It is interim, non-binding and can be revisited. BPC-157 is still not FDA-approved.

Does this make "research use only" BPC-157 legal?

No. The policy only covers compounding by state-licensed pharmacies, federal facilities and licensed physicians. It says nothing that makes "research use only" vials sold online legal for people to use, and it doesn't approve any product.

Which peptides are covered?

Six, listed in Appendix A of the HHS policy document: BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500 (HHS policy PDF). We checked the list against the HHS document on Oct 31, 2026; it matches what NBC News reported (NBC News). Appendix B limits each one to specific forms and routes.

Can I comment on it?

Yes. The notice says written comments can be submitted until 2 weeks after publication, on regulations.gov docket HHS-OASH-2026-0496. Regulations.gov lists the comment period as closing Nov 13, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. ET (regulations.gov).

Is BPC-157 FDA-approved?

No. It isn't an approved drug for any use, and the notice itself says "Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved" (Federal Register). See our FDA-approved peptides list.
