Week of November 20, 2026 · A pill, a mealworm and better sleep

Good news week for peptides. 🎉

Three new studies caught our eye. One is about a pill. One is about a mealworm. One is about sleep.

Quick note: This is education, not medical advice. Every result links to its source, so you can check it yourself.

Heads up: we wrote each summary from the published paper, abstract, or company release linked below. Where a result comes from a company or hasn't been peer reviewed yet, we say so.

A peptide… in a pill?

Psoriatic arthritis is a type of arthritis. It causes sore, stiff, swollen joints. It often comes with a skin rash called psoriasis.

This week, Johnson & Johnson shared results from a Phase 3 trial of icotrokinra. It's a peptide you swallow once a day. It's designed to block a signal in the body called IL-23, which drives inflammation.

Here's what the company reported for 552 adults at 16 weeks:

  • 58% of people on the pill had at least a 20% improvement in their joint symptoms.
  • 41% of people on a placebo (a dummy pill) did.

More people on the pill also saw bigger gains. 29% had at least a 50% improvement, compared with 16% on placebo.

Skin got better too. Among people who had skin psoriasis, 57% on the pill had clear or almost-clear skin. On placebo, it was 14%.

Please note: these are company-reported results, shown at a medical meeting. They haven't been published in a peer-reviewed journal yet. The pill is not approved for psoriatic arthritis. It is already approved in the U.S. for plaque psoriasis (the skin condition).

J&J said side effects matched what's already known, with no new safety issues. Detailed safety data isn't out yet.

A mealworm hid a germ fighter

This one is just plain cool.

Scientists in China used AI to dig through 49,132 proteins from yellow mealworms. The AI chopped them up on a computer, like the gut would, into about 3.8 million tiny peptide pieces. Then it picked 7 to test in the lab.

One of them, called AP2, stood out. In lab dishes, it fought five germs that can cause food poisoning, including Salmonella and E. coli. It worked at amounts as low as one-sixteenth of a common food preservative called Nisin Z.

It also held up when heated, and it didn't harm human cells in the test.

Please note: this was a lab study. It hasn't been tested in animals or people. It tells us AI can find new ideas in unexpected places. It doesn't mean eating mealworms, or any product, protects you from germs.

Better nights for people with sleep apnea

Sleep apnea means your breathing keeps pausing while you sleep. Many people with it also carry extra weight.

Two year-long trials tested orforglipron, a once-a-day GLP-1 pill. They included 712 adults with moderate-to-severe sleep apnea and overweight or obesity. Eli Lilly paid for the trials.

After 52 weeks:

  • Breathing pauses dropped by about 18 an hour on the pill. On placebo, they dropped by about 5.
  • Body weight fell about 10–11% on the pill, compared with 1–2% on placebo.
  • Blood pressure (the top number) dropped about 7 points on the pill, compared with about 2 on placebo.
  • A blood marker of inflammation (called hs-CRP) fell more on the pill, too.

Results looked similar for people who use a CPAP machine and people who don't. The researchers said the pill isn't meant to replace CPAP.

Worth knowing: people also lost a good bit of weight. The researchers think that likely helped their sleep. The pill is approved in the U.S. for weight loss. It is not approved for sleep apnea.

A closer look at heart health

Here's a small but interesting one from Nature Medicine.

Doctors tested semaglutide in 81 people with obesity and a type of heart failure called HFpEF. This is when the heart's main pumping chamber is stiff, so pressure builds up inside.

Researchers measured pressure inside the heart directly. After 52 weeks:

  • During light exercise, heart pressure dropped by about 10 points more on semaglutide than on placebo.
  • 54% of people on semaglutide got to normal heart pressure, both at rest and during exercise. On placebo, it was 7%.

Please note: this is a small study that looked at how the drug works in the heart. It's not a big trial of heart attacks or hospital stays. Bigger studies are the next step.

The fine print (30 seconds, promise)

In the sleep apnea trials, stomach side effects were the most common: nausea, diarrhea and constipation. Most were mild to moderate. About 7 in 10 people on the pill had at least one stomach problem. About 4 in 10 on placebo did.

These results come from specific medicines, in specific groups of people, with doctors watching. They don't carry over to other products.

Talk to your doctor before starting or changing any medicine.
