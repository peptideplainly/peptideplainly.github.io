This week's issue

Week of October 23, 2026 · Five new peptide studies, in plain language

Education only, not medical advice. Every summary links to the study so you can check it yourself.

Each summary is based only on the study's published abstract. We haven't read the full papers, so details may be missing.

1. Can semaglutide help people drink less?

Study type: Review of clinical trials, real-world cohorts, and mechanism studies · Addictive Behaviors
PMID: 42880142

What they studied: Whether semaglutide, best known for diabetes and weight loss, could be repurposed to treat alcohol use disorder (AUD).

What they found: In randomized trials, once-weekly semaglutide significantly reduced alcohol cravings and drinks per drinking day, even in people who weren't seeking treatment. Large cohort studies linked semaglutide to fewer AUD-related hospitalizations. Tirzepatide was also linked to lower odds of binge drinking. Effects looked strongest in people who also had obesity or metabolic disease. Early data suggest these drugs act on the brain's reward circuitry, dampening the dopamine "reward" from drinking.

Why it matters: This is one of the most exciting spin-off areas for GLP-1 drugs. Still, no GLP-1 drug is FDA-approved for alcohol use disorder, and these uses remain investigational.

Limitations: Small samples, short follow-up, self-reported drinking, and possible confounding from weight loss. The authors want trials in people with AUD only.

2. GLP-1 drugs and tendon healing

Study type: Systematic review and meta-analysis (41 studies) · Archives of Medical Science
PMID: 42877202

What they studied: Whether GLP-1 receptor agonists affect tendon healing and tendon-related surgery outcomes, using animal, human experimental, and clinical cohort studies.

What they found: Animal studies generally supported tendon-healing effects, through less inflammation, more fibroblast activity, new blood vessel growth, and tissue remodeling. Pooling two retrospective cohorts suggested a lower risk of revision surgery after total shoulder replacement in GLP-1 users.

Why it matters: Millions of people on GLP-1 drugs also have joint and tendon problems, so a possible healing bonus is worth studying properly.

Limitations: Only two studies could be pooled, they differed a lot, and retrospective data can't show cause and effect.

3. BPC-157 and TB-500 for nerve injury after dental surgery

Study type: Narrative review plus 3 uncontrolled clinical cases · Frontiers in Oral Health
PMID: 42876687

What they studied: Injury to the inferior alveolar nerve (the nerve giving feeling to the lower lip and chin) is a known complication of oral surgery with few good treatments. The authors reviewed the case for "regenerative peptides" such as BPC-157, TB-500 (a thymosin β4 derivative), and ARA-290.

What they found: Lab and animal research suggests these peptides may influence inflammation, cell protection, blood vessel growth, and nerve regrowth. Three patients treated with a combination of peptides, dexamethasone, and local anesthetic regained sensation during follow-up. The authors propose a phased treatment framework.

Why it matters: It's a rare look at these popular peptides in actual patients, and it shows where the evidence really stands.

Limitations: Three uncontrolled cases can't show the peptides caused the recovery, since nerves can heal on their own. The authors stress these peptides remain investigational, with open questions on safety, delivery, and long-term outcomes.

4. What's next for knee osteoarthritis, including GLP-1 drugs

Study type: Expert review · Nature Reviews Rheumatology
PMID: 42875481

What they studied: The fast-changing treatment landscape for knee osteoarthritis (OA), a leading cause of chronic pain and disability worldwide.

What they found: Semaglutide is the first incretin-based drug to show substantial benefits for knee OA pain and function. Other promising signals include GLP-1 drugs injected directly into the joint, the anabolic agent TPX-100, and joint-injected IL-1 receptor antagonist gene therapy. Stem cell (mesenchymal stromal cell) therapies have been inconsistent in phase III trials.

Why it matters: Current painkillers only help modestly, and drugs that slow the disease itself have repeatedly failed. Peptide-based drugs are now part of the most promising wave.

Limitations: This is a review, not new trial data, and many of the options are still early.

5. Even pharmacy students miss GLP-1 side effects

Study type: Cross-sectional survey, 296 Pharm.D. students in Saudi Arabia · Libyan Journal of Medicine
PMID: 42886550

What they studied: How much future pharmacists know about the side effects of GLP-1 drugs used for obesity.

What they found: The average knowledge score was 7.6 out of 17. Nausea was the best-known side effect (59.1% recognized it). Fewer recognized the thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodent studies (25.0%) or diabetic retinopathy complications (36.5%). Only 37.2% knew low blood sugar is uncommon when a GLP-1 drug is used alone. Most students wanted more education on less common side effects.

Why it matters: If future pharmacists find GLP-1 safety confusing, it's no surprise patients do too. It's a good reminder to ask your clinician about the less common side effects, not just nausea.

Limitations: One university, a 26.5% response rate, and a newly developed questionnaire.
