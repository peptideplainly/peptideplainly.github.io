Issue #4 · November 6, 2026

The HHS peptide policy, plainly: what it covers, what it doesn't, and how to comment by Nov 13

Week of November 6, 2026 · The HHS peptide policy, plainly, plus 3 new studies

Education only, not medical advice. Every summary links to the source so you can check it yourself.

Study summaries are based only on each study's published abstract. We haven't read the full papers, so details may be missing.

1. The HHS peptide policy: what it covers, what it doesn't, and how to comment

Source type: Federal Register notice + HHS policy document · Docket: HHS-OASH-2026-0496

What happened: HHS published an interim enforcement-discretion policy for 503A compounding with six peptides in the Federal Register on Oct 30 (91 FR 68526, doc 2026-22192). The six, listed in Appendix A of the HHS policy document, are BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500.

What it covers:
- HHS says it "does not intend to take enforcement action against a State-licensed pharmacy, Federal facility, or licensed physician" compounding with one of the six (Federal Register).
- Only when its conditions are met, including a patient-specific prescription, a bulk substance from an FDA-registered facility with a valid certificate of analysis, a "compounded drug product" label and reporting serious adverse events to FDA's MedWatch (HHS policy).
- Only the forms and routes listed in Appendix B. For example, semax is listed for intranasal use only, and KPV for topical and oral forms only (HHS policy).

What it doesn't cover:
- Blends and stacks. Each product must contain "only a single Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) listed in Appendix A" (HHS policy).
- "Research use only" vials. The policy is about pharmacies, federal facilities and physicians. HHS says it aims to "discourage the continued, widespread use of certain research-use-only peptides supplied by foreign manufacturers that pose a safety risk to US patients" (HHS policy).
- FDA approval. In the notice's words: "Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved" (Federal Register).
- The 503A bulks list. Nothing is added yet. FDA's future rulemakings "may propose" adding some or all of the six (Federal Register).

It's interim: HHS "may revisit" the policy if "a safety signal or product quality issue arises," and it "is not binding on HHS or the public" (Federal Register).

How to comment: Anyone can. Go to the comment page on regulations.gov, click Comment, write what you think and why, and submit. Comments close Nov 13, 2026, 11:59 p.m. ET (regulations.gov). Specific, first-hand comments tend to be more useful than "yes" or "no."

2. Orthopaedic surgeons review BPC-157 and TB-500: "Routine clinical use cannot be currently recommended"

Study type: Narrative review · Journal of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (Oct 27)
PMID: 42937313

What they found: BPC-157 has the most animal evidence for tendon, ligament, muscle and bone, but the human musculoskeletal evidence is "one small retrospective uncontrolled case series." Validated dosing, pharmacokinetic and long-term safety data are lacking. TB-500 is a seven-amino-acid fragment of thymosin beta-4 and is often confused with the full protein, with no proven equivalence. The authors also flag product identity, purity and sterility concerns, and antidoping rules for athletes.

Why it matters: It's a timely counterweight to the policy news. Being compoundable isn't the same as being proven to work.

Limitations: A narrative review, not a systematic one, and it adds no new data.

3. Oral orforglipron in a phase 3 trial in Japan

Study type: Randomised, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial (ATTAIN-J), 238 adults · Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology (Oct 30)
PMID: 42962687

What they found: At 72 weeks, average weight change was -5.9% to -8.7% across the three orforglipron doses, vs -0.6% on placebo. Side effects were mostly mild-to-moderate stomach symptoms, and nausea was the most common reason people stopped.

Why it matters: Orforglipron is a once-daily pill, not an injection, and this extends its data to Japanese adults.

Limitations: Small trial, funded by Eli Lilly (the drug's maker), with Lilly employees among the authors.

4. CagriSema in type 2 diabetes: what six trials add up to

Study type: Systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis, 6 trials, 4,554 participants · Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism (Oct 27)
PMID: 42935221

What they found: CagriSema (cagrilintide plus semaglutide) beat placebo on blood sugar and weight. Compared with semaglutide alone, the extra weight loss was "probable" (-5.84 percentage points), but the extra blood-sugar benefit was "modest and uncertain." Stomach side effects and side-effect-related dropouts were higher than with placebo.

Why it matters: Combination drugs are the next wave. This suggests the main extra benefit over semaglutide alone is weight, not blood sugar.

Limitations: Only six trials, and the comparison against semaglutide was less precise.
